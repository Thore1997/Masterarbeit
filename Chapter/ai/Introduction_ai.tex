Outlier analysis is a critical task in both academic research and industrial applications. 
In recent years, machine learning has been increasingly adopted to automate and refine outlier detection. 
A notable contribution to this field is the InterCont method, an approach based on internal contrastive learning proposed by Shenkar and Wolf (2022). 
While outlier detection is fundamentally a classification task, it is characterized by extreme class imbalance.
Despite its importance, a single formal definition of an outlier has not reached universal consensus; Ayadi et al. (2017) identified twelve distinct interpretations. 
This study adopts the definition established by Hawkins (1980), which identifies outliers—also termed anomalies (Chandola et al., 2009)—as observations that deviate so significantly from other observations as to arouse suspicions that they were generated by a different mechanism. 
These mechanisms may include recording errors, misreporting, or sampling errors. Depending on the research context, outliers may either represent experimental noise to be removed or primary targets of interest that provide critical information (Smiti, 2020).
Outlier detection is vital in high-stakes domains. 


In cybersecurity, it facilitates the detection of network intrusions or malware through unusual traffic patterns. 
In finance, these methods identify fraudulent activities, such as the unauthorized use of credit cards (Rezapour, 2019). 
In healthcare, outlier detection assists in diagnosing rare diseases and identifying anomalous patient data.


Taxonomically, outliers are categorized into three primary types:


\begin{itemize}
    \item   global Outliers (Point Anomalies): Individual data points that deviate significantly from the entire dataset.
    \item   Contextual Outliers (Conditional Anomalies): Data points considered anomalous only within a specific context. 
            For example, a high financial expenditure is normal during a holiday period but anomalous in a standard work week.
    \item   Collective Outliers: A sequence of data points that are not anomalous individually but become so when appearing together, such as a high frequency of small, rapid transactions.
\end{itemize}           

Distinct from outlier detection are related tasks such as Noisy Data Identification, which addresses incorrect or missing values; Novelty Detection, which seeks previously unobserved but potentially valid patterns (Domingues, 2019); and Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Detection, which identifies data points that do not belong to any of the known training classes.

A contemporary shift in the field emphasizes Explainable Outlier Detection. 
It is no longer sufficient to flag an anomaly; models must provide the underlying rationale for the classification. 
This often involves assigning importance levels based on the distance from the "normal" distribution or identifying causal interactions where one primary outlier triggers a sequence of secondary anomalies (Panjei et al., 2022).


High-dimensional datasets introduce the Curse of Dimensionality (CoD), which significantly complicates detection tasks. 
As dimensionality ($p$) increases, the volume of the space grows exponentially, causing data points to become sparsely distributed. 
This sparsity increases the mean distance between points, making the Euclidean distance—a common measure of similarity—less reliable. 
Furthermore, in cases where $p > n$ (more dimensions than samples), perfect multicollinearity may occur, where variables become linear combinations of others, leading to redundancy. 
High dimensionality also increases the risk of overfitting, where models capture stochastic noise rather than a generalizable signal (Altman and Krzywinski, 2018).


This thesis evaluates the InterCont method (Shenkar and Wolf, 2022) against two benchmarks: the k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) algorithm and the Minimum Covariance Determinant (MCD). 
Initial findings by Shenkar and Wolf suggest that while InterCont is innovative, k-NN often achieves superior stability and F1 scores. 
Consequently, this research tests two hypotheses:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item H1: k-NN will achieve the highest predictive performance across the selected metrics.
    \item H2: InterCont will demonstrate superior performance relative to the statistical benchmark (MCD) but remain secondary to k-NN.
\end{enumerate}


The following chapters detail the theoretical foundations of these methods, the experimental design using high-dimensional datasets, and a comparative analysis of their performance and generalizability.
