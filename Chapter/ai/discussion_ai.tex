\section{Theoretical Interpretation}
The UMAP visualization in Figure \ref{fig:umap} reveals that the outliers in the Wine dataset form a highly concentrated and isolated cluster, spatially distinct from the cohesive yet more dispersed inlier distribution. 
This topological formation suggests that the dataset primarily contains global outliers rather than contextual or collective outliers. 
While such clear geometric separation generally favors distance-based approaches like k-NN and MCD, this dataset characteristic also facilitated a highly effective distinction for the InterCont algorithm.


By utilizing a sliding window approach for data augmentation, InterCont effectively captures local feature dependencies—a contrast to the global perspective taken by the benchmarks. 
Although the Wine dataset is characterized predominantly by linear relationships, this localized approach significantly improves the discriminatory power of the method. 
This is achieved through the InterCont architecture, which maps these local dependencies onto a larger feature space. 
Consistent with Cover's Theorem, this projection into a higher-dimensional space enables the method to distinguish even subtle irregularities that are non-separable in the original input space.


In combination with the semi-supervised paradigm, InterCont defines the "normal" manifold with greater granularity than global statistical models. 
The superiority of InterCont is particularly evident in the Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC), where it achieved a mean of 92.93, while k-NN and MCD stalled at 77.82. 
This discrepancy highlights a critical diagnostic reality: while the ROC AUC yields optimistic assessments by incorporating true negatives, the AUPRC reveals that the benchmarks are significantly less effective at identifying the specific minority class.

The integration of an ensemble strategy involving the permutation of dimensions ensures that the model systematically evaluates various feature orderings. 
This prevents critical relationships from being omitted due to arbitrary data structuring, ensuring the method captures the "whole picture" of feature interactions. 
Furthermore, the implementation of a double normalization process—applying both row-wise and column-wise L2 normalization—projects observations onto a unit hypersphere. 
This ensures that classification is based solely on the cosine similarity of the anchor with its positive and negative counterparts. 


In conjunction with the Tanh activation function in the first layer of Network F (which centers the input data), this mechanism prevents data points from being erroneously classified as outliers based on the absolute magnitude of extreme values, focusing instead on the orientation and alignment of feature representations.
Because UMAP is a machine learning method as well and UMAP achieves high distincition between the outliers and inliers, you could argue, that generally the use of machine learning approaches could lead to better performance in outlier detection.


Despite the favorable data structure, the k-NN algorithm yielded the second-highest predictive performance but remained secondary to InterCont. 
Achieving these results required a hyperparameter of k=1, effectively basing classification on the distance to only a single nearest neighbor. 
This finding indicates that the data structure is dominated by global outliers that are sparsely distributed across the feature space, while the inlier class is relatively equally distributed


If the dataset contained cluster outliers, the internal distances within those clusters would likely mask the anomalies, leading to an increased number of false negatives and a decreased F1-Score. 
The necessity of a low k value also suggests that the dataset contains minimal noise; in a noisier environment, k=1 would lead to overfitting and a high false-negative rate. 
The fact that the semi-supervised approach improves k-NN's performance further proves the clear separation of classes. 
By incorporating the characteristics of the "normal" training data to enhance the decision boundary, k-NN achieves better discriminatory performance. 
However, the reliance on k=1 highlights how k-NN suffers from the Curse of Dimensionality. 
As dimensionality increases, the contrast between distances diminishes. When k>1, the resulting mean distance further reduces the mathematical gap between points, blurring the distinction between inliers and outliers.


The MCD estimator demonstrated the lowest performance among the candidate methods. 
Notably, the inherent affine equivariance of MCD, which theoretically eliminates the necessity for data normalization, provided no measurable advantage here. 
The superior performance of non-equivariant methods like InterCont and k-NN suggests that specific mechanics for handling feature interactions are more critical for detection efficacy than geometric stability. 
Furthermore, as evidenced by the QQ-Plot (Figure X), the data is not Gaussian distributed, violating the fundamental distributional assumptions of the MCD estimator. 
Even when applying a semi-supervised approach—estimating the mean and covariance matrix from the normal training data—the estimator fails to achieve a clean separation. 
This instability is reflected in the high standard deviation of MCD's AUPRC results (SD=16.43), indicating that the robust covariance estimator was highly sensitive to the specific data splits.
