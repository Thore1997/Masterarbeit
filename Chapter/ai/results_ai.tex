\subsubsection*{Statistical Inference}
The validation of empirical results necessitates the application of rigorous statistical testing. 
To mitigate the distributional challenges identified, the non-parametric Friedman test will be deployed. 
This procedure serves as a global statistical test to determine whether significant differences exist across the three methods. 
Rather than comparing absolute performance values, the Friedman test utilizes the ranks of the results within each individual run. 

The Null hypothesis is defined as: 
\begin{equation}
    H_0 : R_1 = R_2 = R_3.
\end{equation}

This approach is suitable for analyzing differences between more than two groups. 
The test assigns a rank of 1 to the best performing method within a run and the highest rank to the worst performer, thereby neutralizing the influence of absolute result magnitudes. 
This transformation eliminates the impact of outliers. 


A fundamental requirement of the Friedman test is the independence of the observations. 
This condition is satisfied in the current study because the runs are shuffled and conducted independently of one another. 
Subsequently, the mean rank $R_j$ for each method is calculated.


The test statistics is formally defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \chi_F^2 = \frac{12n}{k(k+1)} \left[ \sum_{j=1}^{k} R_j^2 - \frac{k(k+1)^2}{4} \right],
\end{equation}

whereas, $n = 500$ is the number of runs, $k = 3$ the number of methods. 


The result follows a $\chi^2$ distribution and are displayed in table \ref{tab:friedman}.
The application of the Friedman test across all metrics resulted in $p < 0.001$, which provides evidence that at least two methods exhibit statistically significant differences in every instance.

Furthermore, the standard deviations reported in Table \ref{tab:results_bench} exhibit substantial variation, suggesting that the assumption of homoscedasticity may be violated. 
To formally evaluate the homogeneity of variances, Levene's test is employed to determine whether the variances across the different groups are statistically equivalent. 
The null hypothesis $H_0$ for Levene's test posits that the population variances are equal across all groups, which can be formally expressed as:


\begin{equation}
    H_0 = \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 =  \sigma_3^2. 
\end{equation}


If the resulting p-value is below the chosen significance level, the null hypothesis is rejected, indicating that the variances are significantly different.
The results are displayed in table \ref{tab:levene}.
The results across all evaluated metrics demonstrate a significance level of $p < 0.001$, confirming that the requirement for homoscedasticity of variances has not been satisfied. 


Levene's test and the boxplots in Figure \ref{fig:result_boxplot} confirm substantial heteroscedasticity, the assumption of identical distribution shapes is not satisfied. 
Under these conditions, the Friedman test is interpreted as a measure of stochastic dominance rather than a direct comparison of medians. 
The results are displayed in table \ref{tab:levene}.

This interpretation identifies which method consistently achieves superior rankings throughout the experimental runs, thereby accounting for the distributional variations present in the data.


To further delineate specific performance differentials, a pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank test is conducted. 
This non-parametric procedure is utilized within the paper of \citet{shenkar2022anomaly}. 
The test is appropriate for paired observations \citep[p. 80]{wilcoxon1945individual}, wherein each data point in one group corresponds directly to a specific observation in the comparison group. 
Given that the evaluations are conducted on the same datasets, the observations are inherently linked across methods, satisfying the requirement for dependent samples despite the stochastic nature of individual runs.


The test evaluates the null hypothesis that the median of the differences between the paired observations is zero. 
By ranking the absolute differences between the pairs and assigning the sign of the original difference to these ranks, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test provides a robust assessment of whether one method systematically outperforms another without requiring the assumption of normally distributed differences.

The Wilcoxon signed-rank test involves a structured sequence of computational steps. 
Initially, the difference $d_i$ is calculated for every paired observation between two methods. 
Observations where $d_i = 0$ are excluded from the analysis. The remaining absolute differences $\mid d_i\mid$ are then ranked from smallest to largest, where the smallest non-zero difference is assigned rank 1. 
In instances where multiple pairs exhibit identical absolute differences, a tie-breaking procedure is applied by assigning the mean of the ranks that would have otherwise been allocated to those positions. 
Following the ranking process, the original signs of the differences are reapplied to the ranks, distinguishing between positive and negative deviations. 
These signed ranks are then summed to determine the test statistic, which facilitates the assessment of whether the performance of one method significantly deviates from the other.


The ranks are subsequently summed according to their respective signs, yielding $W^+$ for the sum of positive ranks and $W^-$ for the sum of negative ranks. 
The test statistic $W$ is typically defined as the smaller of these two sums. 
If the values of $W^+$ and $W^-$ are approximately equal, the resulting test statistic will fail to reach statistical significance, suggesting that there is no systematic difference between the methods. 
Conversely, a substantial disparity between the two sums indicates that one method consistently outperforms the other, leading to a rejection of the null hypothesis. 
This comparison effectively determines whether the direction of the differences between the paired runs is consistent enough to be considered statistically significant rather than the result of random variation.


Given the large sample size of n=500, the Central Limit Theorem justifies a normal approximation of the Wilcoxon signed-rank statistic \citep{wilcoxon1945individual}. 
Consequently, rather than determining the exact distribution, a Z-score is calculated. 
The mean $\mu_w$ and standard deviation $\sigma_w$ of the null distribution are defined as follows:

\begin{equation}
    \mu_W = \frac{n(n+1)}{4} , \sigma_W = \sqrt{\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{24}}
\end{equation}

The parameters are used to calculate the Z-Score.
The results are displayed in table \ref{tab:wilcoxon}.
To control the family-wise error rate due to multiple comparisons, we applied a Bonferroni correction \citep[p. 52]{dunn1961multiple}. 
With three pairwise comparisons, the significance threshold was adjusted from $\alpha = 0.05$ to $\alpha = 0.0167$.
Pairwise comparisons between InterCont and the baseline methods demonstrate that InterCont's performance is statistically superior across all evaluated metrics with $p < 0.001$, maintaining significance even after applying the Bonferroni correction. 
MCD in comparison to k-NN shows no statisticall differences regarding the ROC AUC with $p > 0.5$.
